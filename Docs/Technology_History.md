# How technology actually advanced -- and what it means for MO57's progression

Wes, Oct 17 2026: "they should be able to collect pretty much any ore, the actual hurdle with iron is having fire hot enough, so smelting and forging
technology -- do some research into the actual histories of how technology actually advanced and how we got there."

This is that research, condensed into what a realistic progression needs. Dates are the earliest well-attested evidence (many are disputed or revised
as digs continue; the order matters more than the exact year). Sources are listed at the end; every number here is from them.

## The short answer

1. **Getting the raw material was never the hard part.** People mined iron ore with stone tools 41,000-43,000 years ago (hematite for red ochre at
   Ngwenya, Eswatini). Neolithic miners dug flint shafts with antler picks and lit them with chalk-cup lamps (Grimes Graves, from ~2650 BCE). Copper
   Age miners cracked hard rock by fire-setting. Coal was scraped from outcrops and beaches long before anyone mined it.
2. **The hurdle was always heat -- and then knowing what to do with it.** Each metal needed a hotter, better-controlled fire than the last, and each
   fire technology grew out of the previous one: cooking fires -> pottery firing -> kilns -> charcoal with forced air -> furnaces. Iron came last of
   the early metals not because its ore is rare (it is everywhere) but because smelting it needs ~1,250 deg C sustained, which "put it out of common use
   until the end of the 2nd millennium BC".
3. **Even then, iron needs forging skill.** A bloomery never melts iron: the ore is reduced in the solid state into a spongy "bloom" of iron and
   slag, which only becomes usable metal after repeated reheating to welding heat and hammering. Steel needs further know-how (carburizing in
   charcoal, quenching).

So for MO57: **ore anywhere, with primitive tools -- and the progression is gated by fire temperature, fuel, air supply and skill.**

## The heat ladder (what each step could reach)

| Fire | Reaches | Why it matters | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Open wood fire / pit firing | up to ~900 deg C peaks, often 600-800 | cooking, fire-hardening, the first pottery | pottery from ~20,900 years ago (Xianrendong, China) |
| Pit / trench kiln | better held ~800-900 deg C | reliable pottery | first purpose-built kilns |
| Updraft kiln | ~1,000-1,200 deg C | fired ware; enough to melt copper (1,085 deg C) and tin (232 deg C) with a good draught | kilns ~6000 BCE (Mesopotamia) |
| Charcoal fire | burns above 1,100 deg C; ~1,260 deg C with forced air | charcoal, not wood, is what made smelting possible: hotter, controllable by airflow, and it supplies the carbon monoxide that reduces ore | charcoal for iron "since at least 2000 BCE" (Kaman-Kalehoyuk) |
| Forced air: blowpipes, then bellows (pot / bag / piston) | the extra 150-300 deg C | "metallurgical iron smelting and welding need so much heat that they could only be developed after bellows were invented"; tuyeres carry the air into the furnace | pot bellows (Levant, Egypt); double-acting piston bellows in China at least by the 5th c. BCE |
| Bloomery furnace (clay shaft, charcoal + ore, bellows) | ~1,200-1,250 deg C; slag melts ~1,100 deg C, iron stays solid (melts 1,540 deg C) | wrought iron | smelted iron ~2200-2000 BCE (Kaman-Kalehoyuk), rare until the 12th c. BCE |
| Blast furnace (water-powered bellows) | hot enough to melt iron with ~4.3% carbon (1,130 deg C in the Wu state ~500 BCE) | cast iron | China 5th c. BCE; water-powered bellows by Du Shi, 31 CE; Europe 1150-1250 CE |
| Coke-fired furnace | -- | iron smelted with coal once the coal is baked into coke (raw coal's sulphur spoils iron) | Abraham Darby, Coalbrookdale, 1709 |

## The materials ladder (how each step built on the last)

1. **Stone, bone, wood** -- Oldowan flake tools 3.3-2.6 million years ago; Acheulean hand axes 1.76 Mya; bone tools 1.5 Mya; prepared-core (Levallois)
   flaking ~315,000 years ago (Jebel Irhoud) -- planning a blade before striking it. Microliths hafted into composite tools in the Mesolithic.
2. **Fire** -- likely controlled by Homo erectus ~1.5 Mya; cooking ~1 Mya-780 kya.
3. **Fibre and string** -- cordage 32-28 kya; sewing needles and the bow 70-60 kya (Sibudu Cave); the spear-thrower by 17.5 kya (indirectly 42 kya).
4. **Clay** -- fired ceramics 28 kya (figurines), pottery ~20 kya; mudbrick with clay mortar 9000 BCE (Jericho). Pottery kilns (~6000 BCE) are the
   furnaces copper smelting grew out of.
5. **Native copper, worked cold** -- copper beads 8700 BCE (Shanidar); cold-hammered awls and hooks 7200-6600 BCE (Cayonu Tepesi); the Old Copper
   Complex of the Great Lakes ~6500 BCE. Repeated hammering makes copper brittle; heating it in a fire and hammering it hot (annealing) fixes that --
   the first heat treatment.
6. **Smelting copper** -- from oxide ores (malachite, azurite: the green and blue stains that guided every prospector) at ~5000 BCE (Plocnik and Rudna
   Glava, Serbia); Timna's mines from the 4th millennium. Sulphide ores (which need roasting first) came later. **Casting** in moulds, and **lost-wax
   casting** 4500-3500 BCE (a lost-wax amulet at Mehrgarh ~4000 BCE).
7. **Bronze** -- arsenical bronze in the Maykop culture, mid-4th millennium BCE; tin bronze, possibly a foil at Plocnik in the mid-5th millennium,
   swords ~3300 BCE. Tin occurs in "relatively few places" (granite country, and river placers panned like gold), so bronze drove long-distance trade
   and careful recycling. Tin smelts and melts easily (232 deg C); the skill is the alloying.
8. **Iron** -- meteoritic iron for ornaments from ~4000 BCE (worth more than gold); smelted bloomery iron ~2200-2000 BCE in Anatolia, still rare until
   the 12th c. BCE; common across the Middle East by 900 BCE, Central Europe ~800 BCE. Iron overtook bronze because its ore is everywhere while tin is
   not (the old "Hittite monopoly" story is no longer mainstream; the Late Bronze Age collapse disrupting tin trade is the current leading idea).
9. **Steel** -- carburizing wrought iron in a charcoal bed and quenching it hardens the surface; quench-hardened steel in a 3rd-c.-BCE grave in Hebei;
   crucible steel (wootz) in south India / Sri Lanka from ~300 BCE; pattern-welding repeated up to 15 times for the best blades.
10. **Later** -- cast iron and the blast furnace (China 5th c. BCE; Europe 12th-13th c.); the finery forge (15th c.); coke (1709); crucible steel in
    Europe (Huntsman, 1740s); Bessemer (1855).

Other crafts on the same ladder: **lime** (burning limestone above 900 deg C makes quicklime for plaster, mortar and whitewash -- a kiln product, and
MO57's caves and quarries are in limestone); **glass** (~1500 BCE, Mesopotamia / Egypt); the **wheel** (4000-3400 BCE) and the **plough** (3500 BCE).

## Mining, as it really was

- **Surface first**: ochre (hematite) dug 41-43 kya with stone tools; coal "scraped off where it outcropped" and "sea coal" picked from beaches.
- **Pits and shafts**: flint mines with red-deer antler picks, bone picks, flint wedges, and oil lamps in chalk cups (Grimes Graves, 430+ shafts,
  from ~2650 BCE).
- **Fire-setting**: heat the rock face with a fire, then quench it so it cracks -- the Copper Age (southern France), Bronze Age Alps and Ireland,
  Roman gold mines, medieval galleries (12th-13th c.), and in parts of Africa and Eurasia into the 19th-20th c. Smoky and dangerous underground.
- **Coal**: bell pits (dig down, work outward), drift mines (follow the seam into a hillside), room and pillar. Black coal burned at Petrkovice
  25,000-23,000 years ago; household coal in China after ~3490 BCE; Theophrastus (~300 BCE) on coal for metalworking; Roman Britain used most
  coalfields; medieval smiths "raised their own coal" (1183) and London banned sea coal in artisans' furnaces for its smoke (1306).

## What this means for MO57

**Done (Oct 17):** every ore can be collected with primitive tools -- mining a rock face or digging yields what the geology holds (coal, hematite,
copper, tin, flint, and the bedrock's own stone; `FMOGeologyYield`), and `IronOreDeposit` no longer demands a metal pick (stone maul, quality 0.5,
like copper). Harder rock should cost more TIME, not a tool gate -- fire-setting is the realistic primitive answer for hard rock.

**Recommended next (the real gate -- heat):**

1. **Every heat station has an achievable temperature**, from what it is built as (open fire / pit / kiln / furnace), its FUEL (wood / charcoal /
   coal later) and its AIR (natural draught / blowpipe / bellows). Recipes carry a minimum temperature instead of only a station type. Today every
   metal process -- copper smelting, bronze alloying, glazing, glass, iron smelting, bloom refining, carburizing -- requires the same generic
   `Forge` station, and the Bloomery building also just provides `Forge` (per the Feb `recipes.db` export -- to verify against the live DT), so a
   copper-smelting hearth and an iron bloomery are the same thing to the game. Proposed ladder:

   | Station | Fuel / air | Reaches (approx.) | Opens |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | Campfire | wood | 700 deg C | cooking, fire-hardening, annealing native copper |
   | Pit kiln | wood | 900 deg C | pottery, charcoal clamps (separate structure) |
   | Updraft kiln | wood / charcoal | 1,000-1,100 deg C | fired ware, lime (900+), crucible copper with a draught |
   | Crucible hearth | charcoal + blowpipes / bellows | 1,150 / 1,250 deg C | copper smelting, casting, bronze |
   | Bloomery | charcoal + bellows | 1,250 deg C | iron bloom (then forging) |
   | Smith's forge | charcoal (coal later) + bellows | 1,300 deg C at the hearth | consolidating the bloom, forging, carburizing, quenching |
   | Blast furnace | charcoal (coke later) + water-powered bellows | 1,400+ deg C | cast iron (late game) |

   Bellows become a REAL prerequisite (they exist as a recipe today), and charcoal a real input (it burns hotter than wood).
2. **The bloom is not iron yet**: smelting gives `IronBloom01`; turning it into `WroughtIron01` is repeated forging at welding heat (time, skill,
   charcoal) -- already the shape of the data (SmeltIronOre -> RefineIronBloom); it just needs the temperature and skill gates to mean something.
3. **Coal**: fuel for heating, lime burning and smithing; NOT a substitute for charcoal in iron smelting until coke (sulphur) -- the Coal01 item
   already says so.
4. **Skill and know-how**: the "genetic memory" lore lets a colonist attempt anything; the skill (and failures: a cold smelt wastes ore and charcoal)
   is what makes the first iron an achievement.

**Coordination:** the station context the crafting queue needs (audit finding #3 -- an authoritative station actor, fuel, active state) is where
"achievable temperature" belongs; logged for Codex in `claude-codex-coop.md` (CL-0027). Nothing of the heat ladder is built yet -- it is a design
step for Wes to approve.

### Draft data for the heat fields (Oct 17; not authored)

Codex's crafting batch C adds the fields: a station building's `ProvidedProcessTemperatureCelsius` (recipe row, `bIsBuilding`), the runtime
`AMOCraftingStationActor::AchievableProcessTemperatureCelsius` (fuel and air may lower it), and a recipe's `MinimumProcessTemperatureCelsius`
(0 = no requirement). Values below follow the physics: what each process needs, not a balancing guess. The recipe list is from the Feb
`recipes.db` export; check it against the live DT before authoring.

| Station building | Provided deg C | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| BuildCampfire | 700 | open wood fire: sustained 600-800, peaks ~900 |
| BuildForge | 1,300 | charcoal hearth with bellows (~1,260 forced air); a smith's hearth runs hotter at the tuyere |
| BuildBloomery | 1,250 | clay shaft, charcoal, bellows: slag must run (~1,100-1,200) while the iron stays solid |

| Recipe | Minimum deg C | Why |
|---|---|---|
| CookMeat | 150 | searing; any fire does it |
| BrainTanHide | 0 | smoke and work, not heat |
| ShapeCrudePottery, MakeCookingPot, MakeStorageJar, MakeClayBowl | 600 | clay becomes ceramic from ~550-600 (earthenware 600-900): a campfire can, unreliably -- a pit kiln is the step up |
| GlazePottery | 950 | low-fire glazes mature ~900-1,000 |
| SmeltCopperOre | 1,100 | malachite reduces from ~800, but the copper must melt (1,085) to collect it |
| CastCopperAwl | 1,100 | pouring copper: its melting point plus superheat |
| AlloyBronze | 1,100 | tin goes into molten copper (bronze itself melts ~950-1,000) |
| ForgeBronze* (axe, sword, spearhead, knife, helmet, shield, greaves), MakeScaleArmor | 1,050 | bronze goods are cast, then cold-worked and annealed |
| MakeGlass | 1,050 | early soda-lime glass: fritting ~850, melting ~1,050-1,100 |
| ShapeGlassBeads | 900 | glass softens enough to wind beads |
| SmeltIronOre | 1,200 | bloomery: the slag must run |
| RefineIronBloom | 1,150 | forge welding heat to squeeze out slag and weld the bloom |
| CarburizeSteel | 900 | carburizing in a charcoal bed (~900-950) |
| ForgeIron*, ForgeSteelSword | 1,000 | forging heat for wrought iron and steel |

With these, a campfire cooks and fires crude earthenware but melts no metal. The Forge, run on charcoal with bellows, does everything through the
bloomery. The missing rungs are a pit kiln (900) and an updraft kiln (1,100, for lime and crucible copper). Fuel matters through the station's
achievable temperature: wood in a forge should stay under ~1,000 (no iron), charcoal reaches the table's values. That is Codex's runtime field
and needs a fuel-to-temperature rule they are designing (CX-0040: "agree the actual" values first).
